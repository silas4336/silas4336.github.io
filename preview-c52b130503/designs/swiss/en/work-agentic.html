The problem

The three lines moved at different speeds: the website iterated continuously, the CRM followed what sales needed, MaiGPT followed a release schedule. The bottleneck was never writing code — it was context switching. Two days away from a line meant rebuilding where I had stopped and why I had decided what I decided. An hour of time difference with the team added to that cost.

So every procedure points at the same thing: take recurring judgment and make it re-runnable.

Auto-generated review pages

After each round

When a round of changes lands, a procedure turns it into a one-page review: a card per change, titled with what changed, opening onto the actual change and my first-pass verdict (accepted / needs a look / noted), with a feedback box under each card.

The three verdicts have defined boundaries: accepted is for changes that carry their own proof, such as reusing an existing style or adding no new dependency; needs a look is for edge cases or trade-offs I am not sure about, and the card has to state what the question is; noted is for background I will not decide on now but will want later. Fixing the criteria up front is what keeps "looks fine" from counting as a conclusion: if I am not sure, it cannot be marked accepted.

The page gets a temporary URL and is pushed to my phone, so I can work through it on a commute. Review no longer has to share a time slot with development — build at the desk, review on the move.

  • Accepted

    The change carries its own proof: an existing style reused, no new dependency.

  • Needs a look

    An edge case or a trade-off I am unsure about; the card has to state the question.

  • Noted

    Nothing to decide now, but background I will want later.

Phone dispatch and daily digests

With the development environment wired to a messaging app, I can hand off a task from my phone, have it run on my machine, and get a report back. Step-by-step confirmation does not work on a phone, so the rule flips to act first and report after — asking up front only when something is destructive or leaves the building. That boundary lives in a config file.

A separate procedure closes the day by collecting what actually shipped — version-control history, task state, document changes — into what got done and what is planned next. That covers the visibility problem of working remotely, without reconstructing the day in a meeting.

  • Act, then report

    Ordinary tasks sent from the phone just run, and report back when done.

  • Ask first

    Anything destructive or outward-facing asks before it runs. The line lives in a config file.

Conventions as procedures

22 of them

Recurring work each became its own procedure: the format and required fields for filing a request, analysis of an existing implementation, end-to-end testing (bring up the environment, drive the browser, produce the acceptance document), updating and publishing the user guide, a daily task-status check.

What decides whether something becomes a procedure is whether I keep having to explain it. Explaining the same thing to a third person is the signal; one-offs stay one-offs.
